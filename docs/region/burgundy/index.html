regionBurgundy

also: Bourgogne

A strip of limestone running south-west from Dijon where pinot noir and chardonnay have been sorted, fenced and argued over for a thousand years. Everything here is small: the hill is 40 km long and rarely 2 km wide, and it carries 100 appellations stacked in four ranks.

Written down by UNESCO World Heritage (2015)

The story Cited

Medieval monks and the Valois dukes did the surveying: which parcel ripened, which drained, which froze. Denis Morelot wrote it down in 1831; Jules Lavalle classified it in 1855; the Beaune agricultural committee formalised it in 1861; and when the AOC laws arrived in 1936 most of Lavalle's first class became grands crus. The ranks on today's label are a nineteenth-century map given the force of law.

How it is done Cited

Four ranks, and the smaller the ground the louder the name. Grand cru takes 2% of production at 35 hl/ha and labels the vineyard alone. Premier cru takes 12% at 45 hl/ha and labels village plus vineyard. Village wines take 36% at 50 hl/ha across 42 villages. Regional Bourgogne carries the rest. A grower may always sell down a rank, never up.

Today Cited

UNESCO inscribed the climats in 2015, which put a heritage listing on a land-holding pattern rather than a building.
